1. Blockade (1975) — The Historical Ancestor

Decades before Quoridor hit tabletop stores in 1997, game inventor Mirko Marchesi designed Blockade (also published in Europe as Cul-de-sac). In Blockade, each player commanded two pawns and had a supply of wall barriers. While more complex, games often lasted longer due to the larger board size and dual-pawn objectives. Quoridor later distilled this into the elegant 9×9 single-pawn format we love today.

2. Malefiz / Barricade (1959) — The Obstacle Track Race

Created by Werner Schöppner, Malefiz (popularly known in English as Barricade) shares the core thrill of wall-blocking. Players roll dice to advance pawns up an upward-branching path towards the finish line. When landing exactly on a white barricade token, the player removes it and can reposition it anywhere on the board to block opponents. While Barricade incorporates dice luck, Quoridor and DuoOrb removed all randomness, turning the mechanic into pure deterministic strategy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Quoridor like Chess?

Yes, both games are deterministic abstract strategy games with perfect information and zero luck. However, while Chess is about piece capture and piece sacrifice, Quoridor and DuoOrb are about path parity and tempo economics (using 2-square walls to create detours while advancing your own token).

What is the goal in games like Quoridor?

The primary goal is to maneuver your pawn from your starting baseline to the opposite side of the board before your opponent does. Players can either advance their pawn or place a 2-square wide wall to delay the enemy.
